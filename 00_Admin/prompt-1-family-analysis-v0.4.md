### Subtopics

1. **Affective computing and emotion-recognition technologies.** This covers modalities (face, voice, text, physiology) and emotion models. Interest in the field grew markedly after Picard's 1997 formulation of affective computing. The basic emotion concept goes back to Ekman in the 1970s. It is the technical base for everything else. [Picard, Ekman: background]
2. **Automated emotion recognition in marketing research.** This is the application of subtopic 1 to ads, products and shoppers. See Bohorquez Camacho, Lichters and Amor's 2025 systematic review of image- and video-based methods [checked]. It builds on subtopic 1 and feeds 4.
3. **Critical studies of Emotional AI / empathic media.** McStay's 2018 book is the anchor. It argues that media technologies able to interpret feelings and intentions produce a form of technological empathy, and it combines empirical research with critique. McStay and Bakir's 2023 handbook chapter frames the main criticisms as "mental integrity" and "unnatural mediation". This subtopic is in debate with 2 over validity and legitimacy.
4. **Emotional personalisation and adaptive messaging.** This covers real-time targeting, timing and tone. McStay's publication list includes work on micro-moments and programmatic ad-tech. Much of the practitioner literature on this is trade writing and needs careful sourcing.
5. **Manipulation, ethics and consumer protection.** Bakir, Laffer, McStay, Miranda and Urquhart (2024) studied UK adults' views on manipulation by emotional AI. It overlaps with 3 and with the regulation note below.
6. **Emotionally intelligent brand agents (chatbots and companions).** A 2026 review links emotion AI to consumer satisfaction, trust and engagement. Bakir and McStay's 2025 paper on companion apps and Character.ai covers the ethical risks. It overlaps with 4 and 5.
7. **Generative AI for emotional content in creative industries.** One proposed framework studies emotional AI used to create marketing content for cultural-event consumers. A Frontiers call frames "affective generation" as creating emotionally resonant content that adapts to human affect. This is the closest fit to a creative-industries group.
8. **Perceived authenticity and AI disclosure in advertising.** A 2026 SAGE Open study found that AI labels raise attitudes through novelty but lower them by reducing authenticity. A 2026 *Frontiers in Psychology* paper tests whether voluntary flaw disclosure offsets the authenticity deficit. A 2026 thesis found the penalty appears mainly when people correctly identify the source. The findings are contested. It builds on 9 and 7.
9. **Algorithm aversion, source credibility and trust.** The psychological mechanisms behind 8, including the machine heuristic. [Dietvorst et al. 2015 and Sundar's machine heuristic: memory]
10. **Emotional attachment and emotional branding.** A borrowed marketing-psychology tradition (e.g. Thomson, MacInnis and Park 2005; Gobé 2001) [memory, background]. It gives the "brand" half of the topic its theory.

**Relations:**
- 1 → 2 → 4 (technology, then application, then personalisation).
- 3 and 5 critique 2 and 4. The debate is over whether emotion inference is valid and legitimate. A review of 1,000+ studies by senior scientists organised by the Association for Psychological Science questioned whether emotion can be read from facial expressions at all. Legal commentary likewise notes little scientific consensus on reliability.
- 7 → 8 → 9 is a largely separate experimental stream. It joins the rest only through "emotional engagement".
- 6 overlaps with 4, 5 and 8.

**Regulation note.**
- Article 5(1)(f) of the EU AI Act prohibits inferring emotions from biometric data in the workplace and in education institutions, with medical and safety exceptions.
- Brand communication to consumers is therefore not banned outright. Emotion recognition outside those two settings is classified as high-risk under Annex III. The Commission guidelines say text-based inference without biometric data is outside the prohibition, as is call-centre tracking of customers' emotions.
- Article 5(1)(a) (harmful manipulation) and 5(1)(b) (exploitation of vulnerabilities) apply to advertising, and law firms note they complement existing advertising and consumer-protection law.
- [memory, check] The AI Act's transparency duties (Article 50, covering emotion-recognition disclosure and AI-generated content labelling), GDPR and national advertising law also matter. Their application dates may have been adjusted, so verify before citing. Official text: [Article 5, AI Act Service Desk](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-5).

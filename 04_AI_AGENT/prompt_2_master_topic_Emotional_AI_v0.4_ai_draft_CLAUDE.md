0. Scope

Included: how recipients (consumers, audiences) perceive, evaluate and respond behaviourally to emotionally toned content produced or mediated by generative AI in brand communication: ads, marketing messages, social posts, brands' empathic chatbot language. The focus is authenticity, perceived empathy, trust, and the effects of AI disclosure and labelling, including role-specific disclosure.
Excluded: the engineering of emotion recognition as such; deepfakes, fraud and misinformation (consistent with the "fake-*" negative keywords); non-brand contexts (health, mental health, news, politics), which are used only as adjacent evidence.

Field type: mixed. A large experimental consumer-psychology strand sits beside a critical, legal and policy strand.

Definition: the study of how people judge, feel about and act on brand messages that convey emotion when they know, suspect or are told that AI helped produce them.

Commonly confused concepts:

Emotional AI (sensing). This means systems that read and react to emotion through text, voice, vision and biometrics and simulate understanding of affect. It is the critical-studies sense. Here the focus is generated emotional output and recipients' reactions. 
medium
Algorithm aversion. It is a general decision-making phenomenon defined as a biased assessment of algorithms compared with humans. The AI-authorship effect is a narrower, authenticity-driven case. 
Wikipedia
Deepfakes and synthetic media. These fall under a distinct legal category. Ordinary AI-written brand copy is not necessarily a deepfake.
Anthropomorphism / uncanny valley. These concern humanlike design cues. Disclosure effects can arise even when the text is identical.
AI-mediated communication (Hancock et al.) (M). An individual uses AI to talk to another individual. The brand-communication case adds an organisational sender.
Perceived vs. actual empathy. Raters can score AI text as empathic while the label changes how it feels.
Affective ad testing. Brands measure viewers' emotions, which is a different direction of flow.
3. Extended description

Brands increasingly use generative AI to write and illustrate emotionally charged messages: loyalty notes, holiday ads, apology emails, social posts. A recurring experimental finding is a split between two forces. Quality can be good or even superior, but knowing that AI is involved changes the response. In one USC study, AI-written messages made recipients feel more heard than human-written ones, yet people felt less heard once they realised the source was AI. In marketing, seven preregistered experiments found that believing an emotional marketing message was AI-written reduced positive word of mouth and loyalty. 
psu
repec

The effect is not uniform. It is weaker for factual than emotional messages, when AI only edits, and when the AI is openly the signatory. A 2026 study of role disclosure found that more specific statements of what AI did improved ad evaluations in a gradient pattern. Evidence is also mixed outside advertising. A 2026 paper describes the AI-disclosure literature as conflicting, and one Stanford study found that labelling policy messages as AI-generated did not reduce their persuasive effects.

The topic matters because disclosure is becoming law. The EU AI Act's Article 50 transparency obligations apply from 2 August 2026, covering chatbots, AI-generated content and emotion recognition. Brands therefore face a practical dilemma between disclosing and being found out. It also raises normative questions about authenticity, manipulation and what "emotional sincerity" means when the sender is a company. 
stibbe

The main questions are these. When and why does disclosure hurt? Which wording and timing buffer the harm? Do effects fade as AI becomes normal? Do they differ across cultures and languages (including Baltic markets)? How does generated emotion relate to surveillance-based emotion sensing in the same marketing stack?

3. Origins and genealogy

Context. The topic sits where three older traditions meet: emotion in persuasion, human–machine interaction, and authenticity in consumption. It became empirically tractable only with LLMs (from 2022–23).

Predecessors (all from memory, M, unless noted):

Aristotle, Rhetoric (pathos). Emotional appeal as a persuasion mode.
Weizenbaum, ELIZA (1966), and Reeves & Nass, The Media Equation (1996). People respond socially to machines.
Mori, uncanny valley (1970, Japan). Eeriness toward near-human artefacts. The review literature still draws on it: highly anthropomorphic chatbots can trigger the uncanny valley and negative responses through anxiety and distrust. 
wiley
Hochschild, The Managed Heart (1983). Emotional labour: sincerity as performed work, so "who feels the emotion" becomes a theoretical question.
Friestad & Wright, Persuasion Knowledge Model (1994). It is still the standard lens: a 2025 advertising study builds on it to explain inconclusive disclosure effects. 
uva
Picard, Affective Computing (1997). McStay places the origin of computer sensing of emotional life in this 1990s field. 
core
Non-Western parallels (C, no documented influence claimed). Indian rasa theory (emotion as evoked in the audience, not necessarily felt by the author) and Japanese social-robot culture. Both bear on whether sincerity needs a feeling sender.

Founding works for the current line:

Dietvorst, Simmons & Massey (2015), "Algorithm aversion". It showed that people avoid algorithms after seeing them err. Contribution: the core concept. 
arxiv
Castelo, Bos & Lehmann (2019) (M), task-dependent algorithm aversion. Contribution: aversion is strongest for subjective, emotional tasks.
McStay (2018), Emotional AI: The Rise of Empathic Media. Published by Sage in 2018. Contribution: the critical frame for emotion-sensing in advertising. 
ljmu
Yin, Jia & Wakslak (2024), PNAS. Contribution: separates the response effect from the label effect.
Kirk & Givi (2025), Journal of Business Research. Contribution: authenticity and moral disgust mediate the AI-authorship effect. The effect is serially mediated by perceived authenticity and moral disgust.

6. Core concepts (M unless cited)
AI-authorship effect: reduced loyalty and word of mouth when emotional messages are believed AI-written (Kirk & Givi).
Algorithm aversion / appreciation: biased devaluation of, or preference for, algorithmic output. Usage varies; some authors use it for error-induced avoidance only (Dietvorst), others for any anti-algorithm bias.
AI disclosure penalty / label effect: the drop in evaluation caused by the label itself.
Response effect: the quality gain from AI-generated content, separable from the label effect (Yin et al.).
Perceived empathy / feeling heard: the recipient's sense of being understood, validated and valued.
Perceived (brand) authenticity: judged as genuine or true. Reduced authenticity mediates the effects on purchase intent and brand trust in one advertising study. 
researchgate
Moral disgust: an affective mediator in Kirk & Givi.
Persuasion knowledge: consumers' beliefs about marketers' tactics.
Expectancy violation: negative reaction when assumed human authorship is broken.
Anthropomorphism and uncanny valley.
Machine heuristic: "machines are objective", which tends to favour AI for factual content.
Role disclosure transparency: how specifically a disclosure states what AI did.
AI-assisted vs. AI-generated: the EU code proposes a taxonomy of fully AI-generated vs AI-assisted content. 
artificialintelligenceact
Minor-flaw effect (applied to AI ads): voluntary flaw disclosure can restore authenticity and novelty responses. 
nih
Perceived effort / effort heuristic: less perceived effort lowers perceived authenticity.
Disclosure timing: early vs. late disclosure.
Social evaluation penalty for AI use (Reif, Larrick & Soll 2025, cited in an arXiv reference list). 
arxiv
Emotional AI (sensing): the critical-studies sense.
Brand trust.
Moral shame / "punishment of honesty": disclosure penalties persist whether disclosure is voluntary or mandatory (cross-field study). 
researchgate
7. Theories and models
Persuasion Knowledge Model (1994, M). Explains why disclosure triggers scepticism, and why it can also raise trust via attitudinal persuasion knowledge (Koning & Voorveld found both). Limit: does not specify when each path wins. 
uva
Authenticity theory in the AI-authorship effect. Explains the emotional vs. factual asymmetry. Limit: tested mostly on text, mostly in Western samples.
Expectancy violation theory. Explains amplified negativity for affect-laden contexts. Limit: needs a baseline expectation of human authorship, which is shifting.
Algorithm aversion / task-fit accounts (M). Explain why emotional tasks are penalised. Limit: observed effects vary by task framing and moderators.
Uncanny valley and mind perception (Mori; Gray & Wegner, M). Explain eeriness toward minds that seem humanlike but lack experience. Limit: focused on appearance and behaviour, less on authorship labels.
Dual-pathway disclosure models. Authenticity (trust route) and novelty (creativity route) act as parallel mediators. Limit: a single paper, 2026. 
nih
Source credibility theory. Applied to AI disclosure in service ads (lower trust leading to less positive ad attitudes). 
sciencedirect
Regulatory-focus boundary condition. Prevention-focused consumers show a stronger negative link between disclosure and perceived authenticity; promotion-focused consumers the opposite. 
sagepub

9. Key findings or central theses

Weighted to 2023–2026.

Quality vs. label. AI text can make recipients feel heard, more so than untrained humans, but less so once labelled AI. Third-party raters judged AI responses more compassionate than expert human responders, even with author identity disclosed. This is consistent with a label effect that depends on who is evaluating and in what role. 
psu
nature
The AI-authorship effect is stronger for emotional than factual messages and weaker when AI only edits (Kirk & Givi 2025).
Anthropomorphic cues elevate AI evaluations only temporarily and collapse on disclosure, especially in high-stakes contexts. 
nih
Disclosure hurts brand trust and purchase intention in several advertising experiments (three experiments in service ads; an automotive ad study). 
sciencedirect
researchgate
Role specificity matters. Specificity of the disclosure cue, not just the presence of AI, shapes credibility. 
whiterose
Moderators:
Product type: for utilitarian products, disclosure raised perceived novelty and softened the authenticity loss. 
sagepub
Familiarity: consumers with greater AI knowledge view disclosure more negatively (single, lower-tier source). 
researchgate
Timing: delayed disclosure can buffer the negative effect. 
researchgate
Mixed or null results outside brand settings. A 2026 review of 47 journalism studies reported no consistent penalty, and the Stanford labelling study found no reduction in persuasiveness. 
thedecisionlab
Hiding AI is risky. An industry survey reports that over half of respondents would lose significant trust on discovering undisclosed AI ads (practitioner source; treat with caution). 
cint
10. Debates and critiques
Does disclosure always hurt?
Yes: consistent trust penalties.
No, or it depends: labelling did not reduce persuasion in policy messages; disclosure can raise trust via persuasion knowledge. 
arxiv
uva
Status: unresolved; moderators matter.
Aversion vs. a broader "punishment of honesty". Authors argue the trust penalty holds above and beyond algorithm aversion, even when disclosure is mandatory. Status: contested framing. 
researchgate
Is "authenticity" the right mediator? Some work stresses perceived effort, moral disgust or betrayal instead. Status: open.
Mandatory labelling vs. marketing effectiveness. Managers may face penalties for non-disclosure versus weakened effectiveness. Status: now live under Article 50. 
hawaii
Scope of the law. Article 50 requires transparency about AI but does not restrict what AI may be used for, and its deployer text-labelling duty covers public-interest text, so many brand messages may fall outside the visible-label duty (verify per case). 
paulweiss
Critical Emotional AI vs. industry framing. The AI Now report called basic-emotions facial coding pseudoscience, which McStay calls an extreme critique. 
core
Westernness of evidence. Most existing evidence comes from Western samples. 
sissa

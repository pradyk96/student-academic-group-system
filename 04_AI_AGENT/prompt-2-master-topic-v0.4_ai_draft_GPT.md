MODE: WEB
Master Topic: Human responses to AI-generated emotional content in brand communication
0. Scope
Field type: MIXED (empirical and conceptual).

This topic examines how disclosing or labelling AI authorship of advertising and brand content changes perceived authenticity, trust and emotional engagement, including the mechanisms (novelty, algorithm aversion, source credibility) behind mixed findings.

Included: AI-authorship and AI-generation disclosure labels; consumer responses to labelled and unlabelled AI-generated advertising; perceived authenticity; brand and advertising trust; emotional engagement; algorithm aversion; source credibility; perceived novelty; and the psychological mechanisms mediating or moderating these relationships.

Excluded: Emotion sensing and recognition (entry point A); EEG; clinical applications; and regulation beyond its relationship to content labelling and disclosure.

Family: Affective computing and critical studies of Emotional AI.

Research orientation: The empirical component investigates how consumers respond to different forms of AI-generated brand communication. The conceptual component examines authorship, authenticity, emotional labour, human–machine agency, transparency and the social meaning of perceived human involvement.

The topic concerns emotional communication produced or mediated by AI, not the technical recognition or measurement of human emotions.
1. Identification
English name: Human responses to AI-generated emotional content in brand communication.

Lithuanian name: Žmonių reakcijos į dirbtinio intelekto generuotą emocinį turinį prekės ženklo komunikacijoje.

A more consumer-marketing-oriented alternative is Vartotojų reakcijos į DI sukurtą emocinį turinį prekės ženklo komunikacijoje.

Established and alternative names:
- AI-authorship effects in brand communication.
- Consumer responses to AI-generated advertising.
- AI disclosure effects in advertising.
- AI-generated brand communication and perceived authenticity.
- Consumer perceptions of synthetic advertising.
- Human versus AI authorship in emotional marketing communication.
There is not yet a universally accepted disciplinary name for this emerging research area. These expressions identify overlapping research streams rather than exact synonyms.

One-sentence definition:

The study of how consumers interpret, feel about and respond to emotional advertising and brand messages when their production or authorship is attributed to artificial intelligence rather than humans, and how disclosure of that involvement affects authenticity, trust and engagement.

Commonly confused concepts
Concept	Difference
Affective computing	A broader technological field concerned with computational systems that recognise, model or express emotion; this topic focuses on consumer reception of generated content.
Emotion recognition	Identifying emotional states from facial, vocal, physiological or behavioural signals; excluded from this topic.
AI-generated advertising	The advertising output itself; it does not necessarily mean consumers know AI was involved.
AI-assisted advertising	Advertising created through human–AI collaboration, rather than necessarily generated autonomously.
AI disclosure	Communicating that AI participated in producing content; it is an intervention or communication cue, not the content itself.
Brand authenticity	A consumer judgement about perceived genuineness, sincerity, consistency or credibility of a brand; it is not equivalent to factual accuracy.
Algorithm aversion	A tendency to distrust or avoid algorithmic recommendations or decisions in certain contexts; not every negative reaction to AI content is algorithm aversion.

Terminological distinction: AI authorship concerns who is perceived to have created a message; AI disclosure concerns whether and how that information is communicated. These variables should be operationalised separately in research.
2. Extended description
Artificial intelligence increasingly participates in the creation of brand communication. Generative systems can produce advertising copy, images, videos, campaign narratives, product descriptions, social-media posts and emotionally framed customer messages. This changes a familiar relationship between brands and consumers: audiences no longer evaluate only the message, its aesthetics or its emotional appeal. They may also evaluate the perceived identity of the creator.

The central issue is that emotional communication is often associated with human intention, experience, sincerity and personal investment. A message expressing gratitude, sympathy, inspiration, affection or solidarity may be judged differently when consumers believe it was generated by a machine. In this context, authorship becomes part of the message's meaning. Research by Colleen P. Kirk and Julian Givi describes an AI-authorship effect, in which perceived AI authorship of emotional marketing messages reduces positive word-of-mouth and customer loyalty through perceived inauthenticity and moral disgust. [checked] (Kirk & Givi, 2025).

However, disclosure does not produce uniformly negative reactions. It can communicate honesty, transparency and technological innovation. It can also introduce doubts about the sincerity of the brand, reduce perceived human effort or activate negative attitudes towards algorithmic production. Some consumers may interpret the label as evidence of novelty and creativity; others may interpret exactly the same label as evidence of artificiality. [checked] (Kirkby, Baumgarth & Henseler, 2023; Wortel, Vanwesenbeeck & Tomas, 2024; Shi & Jiang, 2026).

The research therefore asks more than whether people like AI-generated content. It examines the psychological processes behind their reactions. Important questions include whether disclosure reduces trust by lowering source credibility, whether it activates persuasion knowledge, whether perceived novelty offsets perceived inauthenticity, and whether the effect depends on the type of emotional message, consumer attitudes towards AI, familiarity with the brand and the role of human involvement.

A further problem concerns the difference between immediate engagement and longer-term brand relationships. An advertisement may attract attention because its AI origin is unusual while simultaneously weakening a consumer's belief that the brand communicates sincerely. Conversely, an AI-generated message might be judged positively when the brand openly acknowledges technological production or when AI is perceived as a tool assisting human creativity. Understanding these differences is important for advertising effectiveness, brand management, marketing ethics and consumer protection.
3. Origins and genealogy
Historical and conceptual context
This research area emerged from the intersection of several older disciplines: rhetorical persuasion, communication research, consumer psychology, branding, human–computer interaction and affective computing.

Its present form is relatively recent because widespread consumer exposure to generative AI advertising created a new question: does knowing that a brand message was generated by AI change how audiences interpret its emotional meaning?

The genealogy below identifies intellectual predecessors and comparable traditions. It does not imply that classical rhetorical or aesthetic theories directly influenced contemporary AI-disclosure experiments.
Predecessors and intellectual foundations
1. Classical rhetoric: ethos, pathos and logos
- Author: Aristotle.
- Work: Rhetoric.
- Period: approximately the fourth century BCE. [memory]
- Contribution: provides a framework for understanding persuasion through the credibility of the speaker (ethos), emotional appeal (pathos) and reasoning (logos).
- Relevance: helps explain why the perceived identity and credibility of the producer may change the reception of an otherwise identical emotional message.
2. South Asian aesthetic theory: rasa
- Tradition: Sanskrit aesthetic theory associated with Bharata's Natyasastra and subsequent commentators.
- Work: Natyasastra, attributed to Bharata.
- Date: uncertain; the chronology and development of the text require specialist verification. [memory]
- Contribution: examines the aesthetic experience of emotion communicated through artistic representation and received by an audience.
- Relevance: offers a non-Western comparative framework for studying how emotional experience can emerge from representations rather than from direct personal experience of the creator.
- Limitation: rasa theory is not an early theory of AI disclosure, advertising trust or source attribution.
3. Communication and source credibility
- Authors: Carl I. Hovland and Walter Weiss.
- Work: The Influence of Source Credibility on Communication Effectiveness (1951).
- Contribution: investigates how the perceived trustworthiness of a communication source influences persuasion and how source information can become separated from message memory.
- Relevance: establishes an important foundation for examining whether an AI label changes the credibility attributed to brand communication. [checked] (Oxford Academic).
4. Persuasion Knowledge Model
- Authors: Marian Friestad and Peter Wright.
- Work: The Persuasion Knowledge Model: How People Cope with Persuasion Attempts (1994).
- Contribution: explains how consumers develop knowledge about persuasion tactics and use it to interpret marketers' motives and influence attempts.
- Relevance: disclosure of AI involvement can make consumers reconsider why and how a brand is communicating, potentially activating critical evaluation of its intentions. [checked] (Journal of Consumer Research).
5. Human–computer interaction and social responses to machines
- Authors: Clifford Nass and Youngme Moon.
- Work: Machines and Mindlessness: Social Responses to Computers (2000).
- Contribution: demonstrates that people can apply social expectations and interaction conventions to computers.
- Relevance: provides a foundation for investigating whether a non-human communicative source can be evaluated through social and interpersonal expectations. [checked] (Journal of Social Issues).
6. Brand authenticity research
- Author: Michael B. Beverland.
- Work: Crafting Brand Authenticity: The Case of Luxury Wines (2005).
- Contribution: examines how brands construct and communicate authenticity through narratives, practices and associations.
- Relevance: explains why perceived sincerity, origin, history and human involvement can matter to brand relationships. [checked] (Journal of Management Studies).
- Authors: Florian Morhart, Lucia Malär, Amélie Guèvremont, Fabienne Girardin and Bianca Grohmann.
- Work: Brand Authenticity: An Integrative Framework and Measurement Scale (2015).
- Contribution: develops an integrative conceptualisation and measurement approach to brand authenticity.
- Relevance: offers a useful basis for operationalising authenticity as an empirical outcome of AI authorship and disclosure. [checked] (Journal of Consumer Psychology).
7. Algorithm aversion and appreciation
- Authors: Berkeley J. Dietvorst, Joseph P. Simmons and Cade Massey.
- Work: Algorithm Aversion: People Erroneously Avoid Algorithms After Seeing Them Err (2015).
- Contribution: investigates people's tendency to lose confidence in algorithmic predictions after observing errors.
- Relevance: provides a theoretical explanation for negative responses to algorithmic involvement, although advertising authorship and algorithmic prediction are distinct contexts. [checked] (Journal of Experimental Psychology: General).
- Authors: Jennifer Logg, Julia Minson and Don Moore.
- Work: Algorithm Appreciation: People Prefer Algorithmic to Human Judgment (2019).
- Contribution: demonstrates that responses to algorithmic judgement can also be favourable; consumers do not universally prefer human decisions.
- Relevance: helps prevent an overly simplistic explanation of all negative AI responses as universal algorithm aversion. [checked] (Management Science).
8. Affective computing and Emotional AI
- Author: Rosalind W. Picard.
- Work: Affective Computing (1997).
- Contribution: establishes a foundational framework for computational systems that recognise, model and express emotion.
- Relevance: provides the technological and intellectual family context, although the present topic is concerned with responses to generated emotional content rather than emotion-recognition systems. [checked] (MIT Press).
- Author: Andrew McStay.
- Work: Emotional AI: The Rise of Empathic Media (2018).
- Contribution: critically examines technologies that interpret or respond to human emotional life and their social implications.
- Relevance: provides a critical perspective on simulated empathy, emotional communication and the social consequences of technological mediation. [checked] (SAGE Publications).
Foundational transition into the contemporary topic
The convergence of these traditions produced the contemporary research question: when a brand uses AI to communicate emotionally, does disclosing this authorship change the audience's perception of sincerity and the relationship between the brand and consumer?

An important early contemporary contribution is Arango, Singaraju and Niininen's Consumer Responses to AI-Generated Charitable Giving Ads (2023). The authors investigate how knowledge of AI-generated advertising content affects potential donors' responses in charitable communication. [checked] (Journal of Advertising).

The research became more directly connected to brand authorship and disclosure through Kirkby, Baumgarth and Henseler's To Disclose or Not Disclose, Is No Longer the Question – Effect of AI-Disclosed Brand Voice on Brand Authenticity and Attitude (2023). Its central contribution is an experimental examination of AI-disclosed brand voice and perceived authenticity, demonstrating that disclosure does not necessarily reduce authenticity. [checked] (Journal of Product & Brand Management).

These works mark the transition from general theories of persuasion and machine interaction towards the specific investigation of generative AI authorship in contemporary brand communication.
4. Development over time
The development can be understood through five phases. Older works provide theoretical background; the most relevant contemporary research is concentrated in the period from 2023 onward.
Phase 1. Foundations of persuasion, credibility and authenticity — background (1951–2015)
Main shift: From studying the effect of human communication sources to understanding how source credibility and authenticity influence audience evaluation.

Important works:
- Hovland and Weiss (1951), The Influence of Source Credibility on Communication Effectiveness. Establishes the relationship between source credibility and persuasion.
- Friestad and Wright (1994), The Persuasion Knowledge Model. Explains consumers' interpretation of persuasive intent.
- Beverland (2005), Crafting Brand Authenticity. Explores how brands create perceptions of authenticity.
- Dietvorst, Simmons and Massey (2015), Algorithm Aversion. Establishes an important basis for studying distrust of algorithms.
Phase 2. AI, synthetic content and initial advertising research (2016–2022)
Main shift: Research begins to consider AI as an active participant in commercial communication rather than merely an analytical or targeting tool.

Important works:
- Picard (1997), Affective Computing — background, technological foundations.
- McStay (2018), Emotional AI: The Rise of Empathic Media — background, critical treatment of emotional technologies.
- Huang and Rust (2021), A Strategic Framework for Artificial Intelligence in Marketing. Introduces mechanical, thinking and feeling AI as distinct strategic capabilities. [checked] (Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science).
- Logg, Minson and Moore (2019), Algorithm Appreciation: People Prefer Algorithmic to Human Judgment. Provides a counterpoint to algorithm aversion. [memory]
Phase 3. Generative AI and explicit authorship disclosure (2023)
Main shift: Researchers begin examining whether audiences evaluate messages differently when told that AI created them.

Important works:
- Arango, Singaraju and Niininen (2023), Consumer Responses to AI-Generated Charitable Giving Ads. Examines reactions to AI-created advertising in prosocial communication. [checked]
- Kirkby, Baumgarth and Henseler (2023), To Disclose or Not Disclose, Is No Longer the Question. Examines AI disclosure in brand voice and the effect on perceived authenticity. [checked]
This period establishes the distinction between using AI to create communication and informing consumers that AI was involved.
Phase 4. Experimental evidence and conflicting outcomes (2024–2025)
Main shift: Research moves towards experimentally identifying the psychological mechanisms behind consumer responses.

Important works:
- Brüns and Meißner (2024), Do You Create Your Content Yourself? Using Generative Artificial Intelligence for Social Media Content Creation Diminishes Perceived Brand Authenticity. Investigates brand authenticity, automated content creation and human assistance. [checked] (Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services).
- Wortel, Vanwesenbeeck and Tomas (2024), Made with Artificial Intelligence: The Effect of Artificial Intelligence Disclosures in Instagram Advertisements on Consumer Attitudes. Tests the effect of disclosure on advertising attitudes, brand attitudes and source credibility. [checked] (Emerging Media).
- Baek, Kim and Kim (published online 2024; journal issue 2026), Effect of Disclosing AI-Generated Content on Prosocial Advertising Evaluation. Examines disclosure, perceived credibility and donation intentions. [checked] (International Journal of Advertising).
- Kirk and Givi (2025), The AI-Authorship Effect. Examines emotional marketing messages, authenticity, moral disgust and customer loyalty. [checked] (Journal of Business Research).
- Chen, Wang and Hao (2025), AI in the Spotlight: The Impact of Artificial Intelligence Disclosure on User Engagement in Short-Form Videos. Extends disclosure research into short-form video engagement. [checked] (Computers in Human Behavior).
- Bui (online publication record 2025; journal issue 2026), Examining the Effect of AI Advertising Involvement Disclosure on Advertising Value and Purchase Intentions. Tests disclosure through advertising credibility and consumer AI attitudes. [checked] (Journal of Research in Interactive Marketing).
The important development in this phase is the recognition that emotional content, social-media advertising, prosocial appeals and ordinary product advertising cannot necessarily be treated as equivalent contexts.
Phase 5. Mechanism-focused research and disclosure design (2026)
Main shift: Research increasingly distinguishes simple AI labels from more informative disclosure and examines the mechanisms through which consumers interpret them.

Important works:
- Shi and Jiang (2026), Consumer Responses to AI Disclosure Labels: The Role of Novelty and Authenticity. Proposes and tests simultaneous positive novelty and negative authenticity pathways. [checked] (SAGE Open).
- More Than Saying “It's AI”: How Role Disclosure Transparency in AI-Generated Ads Influences Persuasion (2026). Investigates perceived transparency, creation-process credibility and disclosure framing. [checked] (Wiley).
- When AI Meets Advertising Creative: Attitudinal Effects of AI-Generation Disclosures for Copy and Visuals (2026). Compares disclosure of AI-generated advertising copy with disclosure of AI-generated visuals. [checked] (Journal of Interactive Advertising).
- Frank, Hu and Tanaka (2026), Implicit Algorithm Aversion: How Consumer Characteristics Shape Perceptions of Ads Designed by Undisclosed AI Versus Human Advertising Professionals. Examines implicit detection and consumer characteristics in advertising evaluation. [checked] (Journal of Advertising).
- Generative AI Disclosure in Advertising: From Intent- to Production-Based Disclosure (2026). Develops a conceptual distinction between disclosure about persuasive intent and disclosure about production conditions. [checked] (Journal of Advertising).
Current direction: The research is moving away from a simple question of whether disclosure is good or bad and towards examining what consumers are told, how AI participated, what kind of emotional message is communicated and what the audience believes this involvement means.
5. Schools, approaches and key people
Main approaches
A. Consumer psychology and experimental advertising research

This approach treats AI authorship and disclosure as independent variables that influence attitudes, credibility, trust, emotional reactions and behavioural intentions.

Typical characteristics: randomised experiments, mediation analysis, moderation analysis and comparisons between AI, human and hybrid content.

Representative researchers: Colleen P. Kirk, Julian Givi, Caroline Wortel, Ini Vanwesenbeeck, Frédéric Tomas, Tae Hyun Baek, Jungkeun Kim and Jeong Hyun Kim.

B. Brand authenticity and brand identity research

This approach investigates the extent to which human authorship, human effort, perceived sincerity and consistency contribute to brand authenticity.

It is particularly concerned with the distinction between a technically effective message and one perceived as genuinely representing the brand.

Representative researchers: Alexandra Kirkby, Carsten Baumgarth, Jörg Henseler, Jasper David Brüns and Martin Meißner.

C. Algorithm aversion and human–AI interaction

This approach examines whether consumers attribute inferior creativity, emotional capacity, judgement or credibility to AI.

It distinguishes explicit algorithm aversion, triggered by disclosure, from implicit algorithm aversion, which may arise when people recognise characteristics of algorithmically generated content without receiving an authorship label.

Representative researchers: Berkeley Dietvorst, Joseph Simmons, Cade Massey, Noah Castelo, and the more recent work of Björn Frank and colleagues.

D. Critical Emotional AI and media studies

This approach examines the broader meanings of simulated emotion, automated empathy, human agency and technological mediation.

It questions whether machine-generated emotional expression should be understood as emotional communication in the same sense as human expression, and whether audiences can distinguish simulation from experience.

Representative researchers: Andrew McStay, Rosalind W. Picard and Lisa Feldman Barrett.

E. AI marketing strategy and service research

This approach places generative content in the larger transformation of marketing, personalisation, customer relationships and service encounters.

Representative researchers: Ming-Hui Huang, Roland T. Rust and Xueming Luo.

6. Core concepts
Concept	Definition and relevance
AI disclosure	Explicit communication that AI was involved in producing or modifying content. Disclosure may identify text, visuals, video or the overall creative process.
AI authorship	Attribution of creative responsibility to an AI system rather than a human creator. It is a perceived attribution as well as a question of actual production.
AI-generated advertising	Advertising content created substantially by generative AI systems.
AI-assisted advertising	Human-produced content developed with AI assistance, editing, suggestions or generation of individual components.
AI labelling	A visible or otherwise accessible notice identifying content as AI-generated or AI-assisted. It differs from technical provenance metadata.
Perceived authenticity	The audience's judgement that a message or brand is genuine, sincere, consistent and faithful to its claimed identity. Different authors operationalise it at message, brand or creator level.
Brand trust	Consumer confidence in the brand's reliability, honesty and intentions. It is distinct from liking an individual advertisement.
Source credibility	Perceived trustworthiness and expertise of a communicator or message source. In AI communication, the perceived source may be the brand, a person, an AI system or a combination.
Emotional engagement	Emotional involvement with a message, brand or communication experience. It may include attention, sympathy, identification, enjoyment and emotional connection. Its measurement differs across disciplines.
Algorithm aversion	Negative responses to algorithmic systems or outcomes, including reduced reliance or preference for human judgement in particular circumstances.
Algorithm appreciation	Preference for algorithmic judgement or performance in contexts in which users perceive algorithms as effective or advantageous.
Perceived novelty	The extent to which content seems new, innovative, unusual or creatively distinctive. AI disclosure can itself create novelty.
Moral disgust	A negative moral-emotional response to an action perceived as inappropriate or insincere. Kirk and Givi investigate it as a pathway in AI-authored emotional marketing messages.
Persuasion knowledge	Consumers' knowledge and beliefs about marketing persuasion techniques, motives and tactics.
Perceived human effort	An inference about how much human work, attention or personal investment went into producing a message.
Human–AI co-creation	Collaborative content production in which humans and AI jointly contribute to the output.
Anthropomorphism	The attribution of human characteristics, motives or capacities to non-human entities, including AI.
Emotional authenticity	The perception that an emotional expression reflects genuine feeling or meaningful intention rather than merely simulated expression.
Disclosure transparency	The perceived clarity and completeness of information about AI's role, rather than merely the presence of a generic AI label.

Important distinctions across authors: Authenticity may refer to a brand's consistency, the sincerity of an individual message or the assumed human origin of emotional expression. These are related but non-identical constructs. Similarly, engagement may mean interaction intention, emotional involvement or actual behavioural participation. Comparisons across studies must account for these operational differences.
7. Theories and models
7.1. Source Credibility Theory
What it explains: How the perceived expertise and trustworthiness of the message source affect persuasion. Applied to AI advertising, the disclosure label can alter the perceived credibility of the creator or the production process.

Limitations: Traditional source credibility models were developed mainly around human communicators. They do not fully account for distributed authorship among brands, employees, AI models and creative agencies.

Foundational reference: Hovland and Weiss (1951), The Influence of Source Credibility on Communication Effectiveness.
7.2. Persuasion Knowledge Model
What it explains: How disclosure can make consumers recognise marketing tactics and reconsider the advertiser's intentions.

An AI label may lead a consumer to ask whether the brand is communicating sincerely, trying to reduce production costs or attempting to simulate human empathy.

Limitations: Disclosure does not always create negative persuasion knowledge. Consumers may instead interpret transparency as evidence of honesty and responsible communication.

Foundational reference: Friestad and Wright (1994), The Persuasion Knowledge Model.
7.3. Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM)
What it explains: How consumers process persuasive messages through more effortful evaluation of arguments or more peripheral cues.

An AI disclosure can become a salient cue that changes how consumers process the message, potentially shifting attention away from emotional content towards the origin of the advertisement.

Limitations: The model does not by itself establish whether AI disclosure will lead to positive or negative evaluations. The result depends on motivation, ability, context and individual characteristics.

Foundational reference: Petty and Cacioppo (1986), The Elaboration Likelihood Model of Persuasion.
7.4. Heuristic–Systematic Model
What it explains: Consumers may evaluate AI-generated communication using simple cues, such as the AI label or brand reputation, or examine the content and its reasoning more systematically.

Limitations: It is difficult to identify from observed advertising attitudes alone whether consumers actually processed an advertisement heuristically or systematically.

Useful background: Chaiken (1980), Heuristic Versus Systematic Information Processing and the Use of Source Versus Message Cues in Persuasion. [checked] (Journal of Personality and Social Psychology).
7.5. Algorithm Aversion and Algorithm Appreciation
What they explain: Why consumers may prefer human creative work or decision-making to algorithmically generated alternatives, and why that preference can change across tasks, experiences and audiences.

Limitations: Most foundational research concerns algorithmic advice, predictions or decisions rather than emotional brand messages. The transfer to advertising needs context-specific evidence.

Key references: Dietvorst, Simmons and Massey (2015), Algorithm Aversion; Castelo, Bos and Lehmann (2019), Task-Dependent Algorithm Aversion.
7.6. Brand Authenticity Framework
What it explains: How audiences judge brand communication in terms of genuineness, continuity, sincerity and alignment with an established identity.

Limitations: Authenticity is not one universally agreed construct. Consumers can perceive brand identity as genuine even when its communication uses technological production methods.

Key references: Beverland (2005), Crafting Brand Authenticity; Morhart et al. (2015), Brand Authenticity: An Integrative Framework and Measurement Scale.
7.7. Novelty–Authenticity Dual-Pathway Model
What it explains: Why AI disclosure can create simultaneous positive and negative responses. Novelty may increase advertising interest and attitude, whereas reduced perceived authenticity may weaken consumer evaluations.

Shi and Jiang (2026) explicitly investigate these parallel pathways and their variation by consumer regulatory focus and product type. [checked]

Limitations: The framework is relatively new and requires replication in other markets, emotional message types and brand contexts.

Reference: Shi and Jiang (2026).
7.8. Authenticity and Moral Disgust Mechanism
What it explains: Why an AI-authored emotional message might be perceived as morally insincere, thereby producing negative emotional responses and weakening customer loyalty.

Kirk and Givi's model links perceived inauthenticity and moral disgust to reduced positive word-of-mouth and loyalty. [checked]

Limitations: The finding should not be generalised automatically to factual content, editing assistance or contexts in which AI authorship is expected.

Reference: Kirk and Givi (2025).
7.9. Anthropomorphism and Social Response to Computers
What it explains: People may interpret technology through human social expectations. Perceived human-like AI characteristics may make disclosure less damaging or increase consumers' willingness to accept machine-produced communication.

Limitations: Human-like presentation is not equivalent to genuine human emotion, and anthropomorphism can create unrealistic expectations.

Reference: Nass and Moon (2000), Machines and Mindlessness.
7.10. Feeling AI and Strategic AI Marketing
What it explains: Huang and Rust distinguish mechanical, thinking and feeling AI as different capabilities within marketing strategy. Feeling AI is especially relevant to emotionally framed customer interaction and relational positioning.

Limitations: This is a broad strategic framework, not a direct predictive model of how AI authorship labels affect emotional advertising.

Reference: Huang and Rust (2021).
8. Methods and approaches of inquiry
Field type: mixed.

Empirical methods predominate in research directly investigating disclosure effects. Conceptual and critical approaches provide explanations of authorship, authenticity, emotional labour and human–AI relationships.
A. Empirical methods
1. Randomised online experiments

The most common design compares consumer reactions to otherwise similar messages under different authorship conditions.

Typical experimental conditions include:
- Human-created content.
- AI-created content with an explicit AI label.
- AI-created content without an explicit label.
- Human–AI collaborative content.
- Different degrees of AI participation, such as writing versus editing.
The important methodological principle is to hold message content as constant as possible while manipulating authorship information.

2. Between-subjects factorial designs

Researchers can vary two or more conditions simultaneously, for example:
- AI authorship disclosure: present or absent.
- Message type: emotional or factual.
- Brand familiarity: familiar or unfamiliar.
- AI role: creator or assistant.
This approach helps establish whether disclosure effects depend on the characteristics of the message and audience.

3. Mediation and moderation analysis

These methods investigate the processes behind observed responses.

Common mediators include perceived authenticity, advertising credibility, perceived effort, moral disgust, perceived novelty and persuasion knowledge.

Common moderators include AI literacy, prior AI experience, product category, message emotionality, brand familiarity and regulatory focus.

4. Field experiments

Actual brand communication on social platforms can be used to investigate engagement, sharing, comments and behavioural responses under more natural exposure conditions.

These designs may improve external validity but can create problems in isolating the effect of disclosure from other platform and audience factors.

5. Surveys and measurement scales

Surveys investigate consumer beliefs about AI, trust, authenticity and emotional communication. They can provide useful contextual evidence but cannot independently establish that disclosure caused a change.

Existing brand-authenticity scales, advertising credibility measures and consumer trust scales can provide starting points. Researchers should test whether their constructs retain validity in AI-authorship contexts.

6. Qualitative methods

Interviews, focus groups and think-aloud studies can investigate what people actually understand when they encounter labels such as:
- Made with AI.
- AI-generated.
- AI-assisted.
- Created using generative AI.
- Human-created with AI support.
These methods are important because consumers may interpret identical labels differently.
B. Conceptual and critical methods
1. Conceptual genealogy

Traces how ideas about authenticity, credibility, emotional expression and technological mediation developed across communication studies, marketing and philosophy.

2. Critical discourse analysis

Examines how brands describe artificial creativity, technological innovation, emotional sincerity and human involvement.

3. Close reading of brand communication

Examines individual advertisements, social-media posts and emotional messages to identify rhetorical and aesthetic strategies.

4. Comparative theoretical analysis

Compares how source credibility, persuasion knowledge, authenticity and algorithm aversion explain different outcomes in AI-disclosure experiments.

5. Ethical and philosophical analysis

Investigates whether AI-generated emotional messages imply emotional understanding, simulated empathy or simply the technical production of persuasive language.
C. Recommended measurement and design principles
A strong empirical design should distinguish at least the following:
Research dimension	Possible operationalisation
Authorship	AI versus human versus hybrid creation
Disclosure	Present, absent or different disclosure formats
Authenticity	Perceived sincerity and brand genuineness
Trust	Brand trust and communication trust
Credibility	Perceived expertise and trustworthiness
Emotional engagement	Sympathy, inspiration, emotional involvement and willingness to interact
Novelty	Perceived originality and innovativeness
Algorithm aversion	Negative evaluation or avoidance of AI involvement
Behaviour	Sharing, recommendation, interaction or purchase intention

Important methodological warning: Purchase intention, advertising attitude, brand trust and emotional engagement are not interchangeable dependent variables. A study may demonstrate a change in purchase intention without demonstrating a change in emotional engagement.

Research should also distinguish the actual production process from what participants believe the production process to be. This distinction is essential for interpreting authorship effects.
9. Key findings or central theses
The empirical literature presents a mixed picture. Negative reactions are common, but they are not universal. The context of communication, the role of AI, the nature of the message and the audience's expectations matter.
9.1. Findings concerning perceived authorship
Kirk and Givi (2025): AI-authorship effect
Strong experimental evidence
Their research uses preregistered experiments to investigate emotional marketing communications attributed to AI rather than humans.

The main findings are:
- AI authorship of emotional marketing messages reduces positive word-of-mouth and customer loyalty.
- Perceived inauthenticity and moral disgust explain the negative responses.
- The negative effect is weaker when the communication is factual rather than emotional.
- It is also weaker when AI only edits a message instead of creating it.
- The effect changes when the AI is visibly presented as the speaker.
- When the message is understood to have been copied rather than originally created by its sender, the authorship effect can reverse.
These results are particularly important because they connect emotional communication, authenticity and consumer behaviour rather than focusing exclusively on advertising attitudes. [checked]

Source: Kirk & Givi (2025), Journal of Business Research.

Brüns and Meißner (2024): AI production and brand authenticity
Experimental evidence
The authors conducted experimental studies of generative AI in social-media brand content.

Their findings indicate that AI adoption can negatively affect consumer attitudes and behavioural responses through reduced perceived brand authenticity. The negative reactions are weaker when AI assists human creators rather than replaces them.

This is important because it suggests that the perceived degree of human participation may matter as much as the presence of AI itself. [checked]

Source: Brüns & Meißner (2024).
9.2. Findings concerning explicit disclosure
Kirkby, Baumgarth and Henseler (2023): disclosure need not reduce authenticity

The authors examined AI-generated and human-written brand voice using Adidas marketing texts and an experimental design involving English-speaking students.

Their findings indicate that disclosing AI authorship did not make brand voice or brand authenticity significantly less favourable than human authorship. No negative effect on brand attitude was established in their study.

This challenges the assumption that consumers always equate human authorship with greater authenticity. [checked]

Source: Kirkby et al. (2023).

Wortel, Vanwesenbeeck and Tomas (2024): AI disclosure in Instagram advertisements

The study used a three-condition online experiment involving 161 final participants.

Its findings include:
- AI disclosure directly reduced advertising attitudes.
- Brand attitudes were negatively affected particularly among consumers with higher AI aversion.
- No significant effect on source credibility was observed.
- Inferences of manipulative intent helped explain the relationships.
- Disclosure about AI use in image creation versus text creation did not produce different effects in the tested conditions.
The interesting tension is that disclosure could reduce the inference of manipulative intent relative to no disclosure while still producing a negative direct advertising-attitude effect. [checked]

Source: Wortel et al. (2024).

Baek, Kim and Kim: AI disclosure in prosocial advertising

The study investigates charitable advertising and donation intentions.

Its findings indicate that disclosure can reduce perceived advertising credibility and produce less favourable attitudes and donation intentions. Perceived AI human-likeness can mitigate some negative reactions.

This is particularly relevant to emotional communication because charitable appeals often rely on sympathy, empathy and moral motivation. However, the results should not be generalised automatically to commercial product advertising. [checked]

Source: Baek et al.. The article appeared online in 2024 and subsequently in a 2026 journal issue.
9.3. Findings concerning novelty and authenticity
Shi and Jiang (2026): disclosure as a double-edged sword
Three-experiment evidence
This study is particularly central to the topic.

The authors identify two simultaneous mechanisms:
1. Novelty pathway: an AI label increases perceived novelty, which can improve advertising attitudes, product attitudes and purchase intentions.
2. Authenticity pathway: the same label can reduce perceived authenticity, which weakens those outcomes.
Their further experiments identify differences associated with regulatory focus and product type. Promotion-focused consumers respond more positively to novelty, while the negative authenticity pathway is less pronounced in the conditions favouring utilitarian products.

The central contribution is an explanation of how disclosure can generate opposing psychological responses at the same time. [checked]

Source: Shi & Jiang (2026).
9.4. Findings concerning disclosure transparency and creative involvement
Role disclosure transparency (2026)

The study More Than Saying “It's AI” distinguishes between simply identifying AI involvement and communicating the role played by AI in producing an advertisement.

It reports that greater perceived disclosure transparency improves credibility attributed to the advertising creation process and subsequently improves advertising attitudes. It also examines disclosure motive and regulatory-compliance signals.

This suggests that the informational quality of a disclosure may matter more than the mere presence of a label. [checked]

Source: Wiley, DOI 10.1002/mar.70175.

Disclosure of AI-generated copy versus visuals (2026)

When AI Meets Advertising Creative distinguishes the disclosure of AI-generated text from the disclosure of AI-generated imagery.

The reported experiments indicate that disclosure of AI-generated copy can reduce advertising and brand attitudes, while the effects of visual disclosure vary across experimental conditions.

This is important because a single general AI disclosure variable may conceal different responses to different forms of creative production. [checked]

Source: Journal of Interactive Advertising.
9.5. Findings concerning consumer characteristics and implicit algorithm aversion
Frank, Hu and Tanaka (2026)
Recent multi-study evidence
This research compares advertising designed by AI with advertising designed by human professionals without explicitly disclosing authorship to participants.

Across a pretest and multiple experiments involving 6,579 consumer evaluations in Japan, the authors report that undisclosed AI-designed advertisements can perform favourably relative to human-designed advertisements.

However, the findings differ according to consumer characteristics. Greater generative-AI experience, higher education, stronger product-category involvement and lower interest in science are associated with less favourable responses to AI-designed advertising.

The authors propose implicit algorithm detection and implicit algorithm aversion as mechanisms.

This work is especially relevant because it demonstrates that AI-related reactions do not depend exclusively on visible disclosure labels. [checked]

Source: Frank, Hu & Tanaka (2026).
9.6. Overall strength and limitations of the evidence
Research proposition	Evidence assessment
AI authorship can reduce the perceived authenticity of emotional brand communication	Supported by experimental studies; context-dependent
AI disclosure always reduces brand trust	Not established; findings vary
Disclosure can increase perceived novelty	Supported by recent advertising experiments
Disclosure can reduce authenticity at the same time	Supported by recent advertising experiments
Human involvement can mitigate negative AI-authorship responses	Supported by experimental research
AI disclosure invariably reduces source credibility	Not established
Consumers always prefer human-created emotional messages	Not established
AI-generated content can be evaluated favourably without explicit authorship disclosure	Supported in particular consumer and advertising contexts
Emotional engagement is affected in exactly the same way as purchase intention	Not established

Overall synthesis: The strongest current conclusion is not that AI-generated emotional communication is inherently ineffective, but that perceived authorship changes the interpretive context in which consumers evaluate emotional messages. Authenticity, source attribution, novelty, perceived human involvement and AI attitudes are important mechanisms.

A further evidence limitation is that advertising attitude, purchase intention and brand loyalty have received more direct experimental attention than longer-term emotional engagement and relationship quality. Findings about these latter constructs should therefore be treated more cautiously.
10. Debates and critiques
Debate 1. Transparency versus persuasive effectiveness
Position A — Transparency is necessary for consumer autonomy.

Disclosing AI involvement allows consumers to make informed judgements about message production. It can reduce uncertainty and signal that the brand is not concealing its technological practices.

Position B — Disclosure can undermine communication.

An AI label may distract from the message and introduce concerns about sincerity, creativity and human effort. Consumers may evaluate the production process rather than the emotional content.

Arguments and current status: Both positions have empirical and ethical support. The main question is how transparency should be communicated without misleading audiences or unnecessarily distorting their evaluations. Research does not establish a universally optimal disclosure format.
Debate 2. Can AI-produced emotional messages be authentic?
Position A — Human experience is central to emotional authenticity.

From this perspective, a message expressing sympathy, gratitude or love may seem less authentic when generated by a system that does not possess human lived experience.

Position B — Authenticity is a feature of communication and audience interpretation.

A recipient may judge a message as meaningful, appropriate or sincere based on its content, consistency and usefulness, regardless of whether a human or AI system produced it.

Arguments and current status: This is partly a conceptual debate about the meaning of authenticity, not merely an empirical disagreement. Kirk and Givi's research shows that perceived inauthenticity can have behavioural consequences, while Kirkby and colleagues demonstrate that AI-disclosed brand voice does not always produce a negative authenticity judgement. [checked]
Debate 3. Algorithm aversion versus algorithm appreciation
Position A — Consumers favour human creators.

Human authorship is associated with emotional experience, creativity, intention and personal responsibility.

Position B — Consumers can appreciate algorithmic creativity.

AI may be perceived as efficient, innovative, consistent and capable of generating interesting material.

Arguments and current status: The relative preference depends on the perceived task, the characteristics of the consumer and the communication context. Recent research also suggests that aversion may occur implicitly, without an explicit disclosure label.
Debate 4. Simple labels versus meaningful explanations
Position A — A visible label is sufficient.

A straightforward label such as "AI-generated" gives consumers the essential information necessary to understand the message's origin.

Position B — Disclosure should explain AI's actual role.

A generic label can conceal the difference between automated creation, editing, human–AI collaboration and AI-generated components.

Arguments and current status: Recent role-transparency research supports investigating the informational detail of disclosures, rather than treating every label as equivalent. [checked]
Debate 5. Emotional content versus factual content
Position A — Emotional messages are more vulnerable to authorship effects.

An audience may expect sympathy, gratitude and inspiration to emerge from personal intentions and human experience.

Position B — Emotional meaning can be evaluated independently of authorship.

A message may still be effective because it is relevant, well-written or helpful, irrespective of how it was generated.

Arguments and current status: Kirk and Givi find that negative authorship effects are attenuated for factual communication compared with emotional communication. This supports investigating content type as an important boundary condition. [checked]
Debate 6. Novelty versus sincerity
Position A — AI disclosure creates interest.

Knowing that a message is AI-generated can make it seem original, innovative or technologically advanced.

Position B — The same disclosure weakens perceived sincerity.

The label may make the message feel manufactured rather than personally meaningful.

Arguments and current status: Shi and Jiang's dual-pathway findings support the coexistence of these effects. The more relevant question is which pathway dominates under different conditions. [checked]
Debate 7. AI as an author versus AI as a tool
Position A — Replacing a human creator is fundamentally different from assisting one.

Audiences may object when AI replaces personal authorship, especially in emotionally sensitive communications.

Position B — The human–AI distinction is too binary.

Most commercial creative production can involve mixed human and machine contributions. Responsibility, selection, editing and approval may remain human.

Arguments and current status: Brüns and Meißner's research supports examining human assistance separately from full automation. This is an important direction for future experimental design. [checked]
Debate 8. Immediate engagement versus lasting brand trust
Position A — Engagement is a meaningful indicator of communication success.

Attention, sharing and interaction demonstrate that an advertisement can generate consumer interest.

Position B — Engagement does not necessarily establish a successful brand relationship.

An audience may engage with a novel or provocative AI advertisement but subsequently evaluate the brand less favourably.

Arguments and current status: This remains insufficiently resolved. Studies measuring immediate advertising attitudes should not be treated as equivalent to studies of persistent brand trust, loyalty or emotional attachment.
Debate 9. Cultural universality versus cultural variation
Position A — Basic reactions to transparency and authenticity may be comparable across markets.

General theories of persuasion, credibility and consumer evaluation provide a shared conceptual framework.

Position B — Cultural expectations about creativity, automation, emotional expression and brand communication may differ.

Consumer experience with AI and cultural expectations of human responsibility can shape interpretations of disclosure.

Arguments and current status: Research from European, North American and East Asian settings provides an initial comparative foundation, but cultural generalisability requires more direct cross-country replication.

The topic has practical relevance wherever brands or organisations use emotionally framed content and where audience responses to human versus AI production matter.

C — Philosophical hermeneutics

The interpretation of a message depends partly on assumptions about its author and context. This tradition could help explain how disclosing AI authorship changes the meaning consumers attribute to previously encountered brand communication.
13. Open questions
13.1. Questions identified in the literature
1. Does emotional content produce stronger AI-authorship effects than factual content?

Kirk and Givi (2025) report that negative AI-authorship effects are weaker for factual messages than for emotional ones. Further work is needed to distinguish different emotional categories, including sympathy, gratitude, inspiration, celebration and apology. [checked]

Source: Kirk and Givi (2025).

2. Why do AI disclosure experiments produce different results?

Kirkby et al. (2023) find no negative authenticity effect in their brand-voice experiment, whereas Wortel et al. (2024) report negative advertising-attitude effects. Shi and Jiang (2026) identify simultaneous novelty and authenticity pathways. The precise conditions responsible for the differences remain important research questions. [checked]

Sources: Kirkby et al.; Wortel et al.; Shi and Jiang.

3. Does explaining AI's role work better than providing a generic label?

Recent transparency research suggests that the quality and detail of disclosure may influence how consumers assess the credibility of the creative process. Further replication is needed across different disclosure formats and creative industries. [checked]

Source: More Than Saying "It's AI" (2026).

4. How do consumers detect AI authorship without disclosure?

Frank, Hu and Tanaka (2026) investigate implicit AI detection and the role of consumer characteristics. Further research is needed to identify which content characteristics trigger this response and whether detection is conscious or unconscious. [checked]

Source: Frank et al. (2026).

5. How much does human involvement matter?

Brüns and Meißner (2024) distinguish AI-assisted creation from AI replacing human creators. Kirk and Givi (2025) also examine AI editing versus AI authorship. Further work should identify whether human selection, editing, approval or emotional input contributes most to perceived authenticity. [checked]

Sources: Brüns and Meißner; Kirk and Givi.

6. Are consumer responses culturally dependent?

The available research includes European, North American and East Asian settings. Whether cultural expectations concerning human creativity, emotional sincerity, machine intelligence and brand identity explain differences across these markets remains unresolved.

7. Do immediate advertising responses predict lasting brand relationships?

Much research examines advertising attitude, purchase intention and word-of-mouth intentions. More longitudinal evidence is needed concerning emotional attachment, brand loyalty and trust after repeated encounters with AI-generated communication.

8. How does disclosure affect different emotional and visual formats?

The distinction between text and imagery has begun to receive experimental attention. Further research should examine synthetic voices, videos, virtual influencers, personalised messages and multimodal advertising. [checked]

Source: When AI Meets Advertising Creative (2026).

9. What is the relationship between perceived authenticity and actual emotional engagement?

A message can be considered innovative without being experienced as emotionally meaningful. Researchers need to distinguish cognitive evaluation, immediate emotional reactions and sustained interaction.

10. How does repeated exposure change consumer responses?

It remains unclear whether novelty-related benefits decrease as AI-generated advertising becomes more familiar, and whether repeated exposure normalises AI authorship or increases scepticism.
13.2. Additional research suggestions
The following are independent suggestions for the student group, rather than established conclusions from the reviewed literature.

1. The emotional intensity experiment: Compare AI and human authorship across low-, medium- and high-intensity emotional messages. Examine whether perceived authenticity changes as emotional intensity increases.

2. The human-involvement experiment: Compare fully AI-generated, AI-assisted and human-created messages while holding the final content as similar as possible. Measure perceived human effort, sincerity, authenticity and trust.

3. The disclosure-framing experiment: Compare labels such as "AI-generated", "AI-assisted", "Created with generative AI" and a more detailed explanation of the human–AI production process.

4. The Lithuanian consumer study: Investigate whether Lithuanian consumers respond differently to AI-generated emotional brand communication when information about AI involvement is disclosed. Compare results with another European sample.

5. The repeated-exposure study: Examine whether perceived novelty, advertising attitude and emotional engagement change across repeated encounters with the same AI-generated brand communication.

6. The emotional-message comparison: Compare gratitude, sympathy, apology, celebration and inspirational messages. Examine whether consumers have different expectations of human involvement depending on the social purpose of the communication.

7. The source-attribution experiment: Separate disclosure of AI authorship from disclosure of AI-assisted production, the brand as the accountable sender and the human creative director as the final decision-maker.

8. The long-term trust study: Measure whether immediate acceptance of an AI-generated message predicts later brand trust, customer loyalty and willingness to recommend the brand.

Suggested priority: The human-involvement experiment combined with a disclosure-framing manipulation is especially useful because it connects two of the most important competing explanations: authenticity and transparency.

B. What remains uncertain
1. Limited long-term evidence

The contemporary empirical literature is heavily oriented towards short-term experimental responses. Longer-term trust, emotional attachment and customer loyalty require further verification and research.

2. Measurement differences

Studies measure advertising attitude, brand attitude, authenticity, credibility, purchase intention and emotional engagement differently. Their findings should not be treated as directly interchangeable.

3. Researcher affiliations

Some affiliations in the people section represent affiliations at publication rather than verified current appointments. In particular, current affiliations for Yanyun Liu-Thompkins, Frédéric Tomas, Yuting Shi and Zhibin Jiang require additional confirmation.

C. Findings that should be treated cautiously
- Results from individual experimental studies should not automatically be generalised to all products, audiences and markets.
- Disclosure effects may depend on the content being labelled and the perceived degree of human participation.
- Findings about purchase intention do not establish corresponding changes in emotional engagement.
- The proposed mechanisms of moral disgust, implicit algorithm aversion and emotional authenticity require further replication.
